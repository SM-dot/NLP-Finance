# Assignment 3: Measuring the Effect of Iran War Risk on Global Financial Markets

FRE-GY 7871 A · NLP and the Investment Process · Fall 2026

A replication of Rigobon and Sack, *The Effects of War Risk on U.S. Financial Markets*
(NBER 9609, 2003), applied to the 2026 Iran war, with the day-selection step — the one
step those authors did by hand — done with NLP instead.

This folder is self-contained: run the scripts in order to rebuild everything from
scratch, then open `analysis.ipynb`. No data files are checked into git; `data/` is
gitignored and regenerated by the scripts.

## The idea in one paragraph

War risk cannot be regressed on asset prices directly, because nobody observes it: it
is easy to say *when* war news broke, much harder to say by how much it moved the odds
of war. Rigobon and Sack's answer is to identify the effect from a shift in variance
rather than from the level of the factor. If you can name a set of days on which
war-related news was unusually intense, and assume every *other* driver of asset prices
was no more volatile than usual on those days, then the change in the covariance matrix
of asset returns between those days and ordinary days is driven entirely by the war
factor — and that is enough to recover each asset's sensitivity to it, without ever
measuring the factor. Identifying the day sets is therefore the whole ballgame, and it
is exactly the step NLP can do better than a person with a newspaper archive.

## Data and methods

**Sample window.** January 2 to September 11, 2026 — the last trading day for which
both the news and the market series are complete (GDELT's timelines run a few days
behind). That is 173 US trading days, of which the first 38 precede the war: the US and
Israel struck Iran on **February 28, 2026**. Rigobon and Sack had 47 business days and
17 war-news days; this window is longer, which buys statistical power their sample did
not have.

**News data.** GDELT DOC 2.0. The daily measures come from the *timeline* endpoint —
five queries, each returning the whole window in one request and counting every article
GDELT monitored, rather than the 250-article per-day sample the article endpoint caps
you at. (Per-day article collection was tried first and abandoned: GDELT refuses so
large and random a share of those requests that a full sweep takes hours and does not
reproduce. The reasoning is recorded in `src/wikinews.py`.) Event *text* for the
selected days comes from Wikipedia's Current Events Portal — a curated, dated, sourced
chronology — and is used only to label Table 1 and to give the lexicon and FinBERT
something to read. It plays no part in choosing the days.

**The three NLP measures** (`scripts/03_score_news.py`), each standardised and averaged
into one war-news score:

- **attention** — log war-risk coverage volume minus its own trailing 10-day median.
  The *level* of coverage is useless once a war is running: Iran was front-page news
  every day from March. What marks a news day is coverage jumping above its recent
  baseline.
- **direction** — (escalation − de-escalation) ÷ (escalation + de-escalation) coverage,
  where the two vocabularies come from the same escalation/de-escalation axis as the
  hand-built lexicon in `src/warrisk_lexicon.py`. This is the analogue of Rigobon and
  Sack's hand-assigned "Increased / Decreased" column. Only its *shift* enters the
  score: a day that makes war dramatically less likely is as informative as one that
  makes it more likely.
- **contest** — (1 − |direction|) × log(1 + coverage). A day carrying heavy escalation
  *and* heavy de-escalation coverage at once is a high-variance news day even though the
  two cancel in the average. That is precisely the case Rigobon and Sack tag "Unclear",
  and precisely what identification needs.

**Two independent NLP methods, as in Assignment 2.** The hand-built war-risk lexicon
(`src/warrisk_lexicon.py`, phrase-level, longest-match-first, so "peace talks collapse"
scores as escalating rather than as "peace" plus "collapse") and **FinBERT**
(`ProsusAI/finbert`) are both run over the headlines of the selected days, and Table 1
reports both. Off-the-shelf dictionaries are not usable here: Loughran–McDonald scores
financial disclosure language and contains neither "ceasefire" nor "airstrike", and
general-purpose sentiment lexicons read "peace talks collapse" as positive.

**Day sets.** H is the top 18 trading days by the war-news score — the top decile, close
to the paper's 17-of-47 proportion. L pairs each H day with the nearest trading day that
is neither an H day nor adjacent to one *and* that sits in the quiet half of the
war-news score distribution. The last restriction adapts the paper's footnote 7 rule and
is necessary here; see "Three things worth flagging" below. News is mapped to trading
days by settlement convention, so the Saturday the war began lands on Monday March 2.

**Financial variables.** Eighteen, in three blocks. The nine of Rigobon and Sack's Table 2
mapped onto instruments that exist in 2026 (two- and ten-year Treasury yields, ten-year
break-even inflation, S&P 500, BBB and high-yield spreads, oil, gold, the broad dollar);
a global block the original paper did not have (Euro Stoxx 50, Nikkei 225, Tel Aviv 125,
MSCI EM, Swiss franc); and a sector block that isolates the war channel (energy,
airlines, aerospace and defence). VIX stands in for the paper's on-the-run liquidity
premium, which cannot be rebuilt without the Fed's fitted yield curve. Everything is a
daily close-to-close change, in the units of the paper's Table 2: percentage points for
yields and spreads, percent for price indices, dollars for oil and gold.

**Estimation.** `src/heteroskedasticity.py`. Each variable is paired with the
normalising variable and estimated two at a time, exactly as in the paper, under all
three instrument sets (ω₁, ω₂, and both together), with heteroskedasticity-robust
standard errors. Coefficients are scaled to a war-risk increase large enough to raise
Brent by $5 a barrel; the paper's own normalisation (a 25bp move in the two-year yield)
is reported alongside in Table 2b.

## Three things worth flagging about the method

**The equal-sized-sets requirement is load-bearing, not cosmetic.** The paper defines its
instrument over "an equal-sized set of other days". With unequal sets the instrument's
moment condition becomes n_H·E_H[Δx²] − n_L·E_L[Δx²] rather than the difference in
variances, and the estimator is badly biased rather than merely noisier — in the Monte
Carlo in `00_test_estimator.py`, an 18/159 split returns +0.9 when the truth is −3.75.
`src/heteroskedasticity.py` weights each regime by 1/n so the moment condition is right
either way; the day sets are built equal-sized regardless.

**The paper's normalising variable does not work in 2026.** Rigobon and Sack anchor
everything on the two-year Treasury yield, whose variance rose 6.2× on their war-news
days. In 2026 the two-year's variance is 1.05× higher on war-news days and the
ten-year's is *lower*: a war transmitted as an oil-supply shock pushes yields down
through flight to quality and up through the inflation outlook, and the two roughly
cancel. Anchoring on a variable that barely responds puts a number near zero in the
estimator's denominator — the rank-condition failure Rigobon (2003) warns about, and
visible in Table 2b as coefficients that are enormous rather than small, with
t-statistics near zero. Brent, whose variance rises 6.2×, takes the two-year yield's
role. Both are reported, because the comparison is itself a result.

**The footnote 7 rule needs adapting to a running war.** The paper picks comparison days
"as close as possible to" the war-news days, so other factors' variances stay comparable.
Its window was the *run-up* to a war, where nearby days really were quiet. 2026 contains
six months of running war, and the days nearest a big war-news day are themselves war
days — the literal rule made the comparison set *more* volatile than the war-news set and
broke identification outright. Restricting comparison days to the quiet half of the
news-score distribution, then taking the nearest qualifying day, preserves both of the
paper's goals.

**A transposition in the paper's own Table 2.** The text derives IV-with-ω₁ as equation
(7) and IV-with-ω₂ as equation (6); the Table 2 column headers pair them the other way
round. Columns here are labelled by instrument, which is unambiguous.
